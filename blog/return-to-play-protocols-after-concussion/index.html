Return-to-Play Protocols After Concussion: A Step-by-Step Guide

February 08, 2026 — Concussion Therapy

Returning to athletic activity after a concussion requires a structured, step-by-step approach that progressively increases the demands placed on the brain while monitoring for any recurrence of symptoms. The graduated return-to-play protocol, now adopted by virtually all major sports organizations worldwide, provides this structure. Understanding each step of the protocol, and why each step matters, helps athletes, parents, coaches, and clinicians navigate the return-to-play process safely and effectively.

The Six-Step Protocol

The internationally recognized return-to-play protocol consists of six progressive steps, each increasing the level of physical and cognitive demand. The athlete must be symptom-free for at least 24 hours at each step before progressing to the next. If symptoms return at any step, the athlete drops back to the previous symptom-free step and waits 24 hours before attempting to progress again.

Step one is complete physical and cognitive rest until acute symptoms resolve. This initial rest period, which typically lasts 24 to 48 hours, allows the brain to begin its initial recovery. Extended complete rest beyond 48 hours is no longer recommended by most guidelines, as prolonged inactivity can delay recovery and contribute to deconditioning. Step two introduces light aerobic exercise, such as walking or stationary cycling at low intensity. The purpose is to increase the heart rate without straining the neck or subjecting the head to impact. Sessions typically last 10 to 15 minutes.

Step three involves sport-specific exercise that increases intensity but still avoids head impact and high-risk activities. A soccer player might do running drills but not head the ball. A hockey player might skate but not participate in contact drills. Step four introduces non-contact training drills of greater complexity, including passing, route running, and coordinated team exercises. This step introduces the cognitive demands of sport-specific decision-making and multi-tasking.

Step five is full-contact practice, where the athlete participates in normal training activities with the team. This step requires medical clearance from a physician before proceeding. Step six is the return to full competition. Each step requires a minimum of 24 hours symptom-free before the athlete advances.

Why the Gradual Approach Matters

The graduated return-to-play protocol exists because the recovering brain is vulnerable. Returning to full activity too quickly risks exacerbating the original injury, prolonging recovery, and in rare but devastating cases, suffering a second impact before the brain has fully recovered from the first. Second impact syndrome, while uncommon, can result in rapid and catastrophic brain swelling that can be fatal. The graduated protocol is designed to identify athletes who are not yet ready for increased demands before they are placed in situations where further injury could occur.

Each step of the protocol tests the brain's ability to handle a specific level of physiological stress. Light aerobic exercise increases heart rate and blood pressure, challenging cerebrovascular regulation. Sport-specific exercise adds the cognitive demands of motor planning and coordination. Non-contact drills introduce decision-making under physical stress. Full-contact practice adds the physical forces of collision. By testing these increasing demands one at a time, the protocol identifies residual vulnerability before the athlete is exposed to the full demands of competition.

Supporting Recovery During the Protocol

Drug-free therapies can support the athlete's recovery during the return-to-play progression. Microcurrent therapy may help resolve persistent symptoms and promote neurological healing. Cold therapy can manage headaches and neck pain that often accompany concussion recovery. Physical therapy addresses cervical spine dysfunction and vestibular impairment that can cause dizziness and balance problems. These supportive therapies can help athletes move through the return-to-play steps more efficiently while ensuring that progression is driven by genuine recovery rather than symptom suppression.

The Role of the Healthcare Provider

Medical clearance from a qualified healthcare provider is an essential component of the return-to-play process. While initial progression through the early steps may be supervised by an athletic trainer, medical clearance by a physician is required before the athlete is permitted to return to full-contact practice and competition. This clinical evaluation should include a comprehensive symptom assessment, neurocognitive testing, balance assessment, and any additional evaluations indicated by the athlete's specific injury and recovery pattern.

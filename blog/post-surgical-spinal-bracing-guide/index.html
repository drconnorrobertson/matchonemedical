Post-Surgical Spinal Bracing: What to Expect and How to Succeed

January 02, 2026 — Back Bracing

Receiving a back brace as part of your post-surgical recovery plan can feel overwhelming. The device may seem restrictive, the instructions may feel complex, and the prospect of wearing a brace for weeks or months may be daunting. However, post-surgical bracing plays a critical role in protecting your surgical repair, allowing tissues to heal properly, and ultimately achieving the best possible outcome from your procedure. Understanding why you need the brace, how to wear it properly, and what to expect during your bracing period makes the entire experience more manageable.

Why Post-Surgical Bracing Is Prescribed

After spinal surgery, the structures of the spine need time to heal and stabilize. Whether your procedure involved a fusion, decompression, disc replacement, or repair of a fracture, the surgical site is vulnerable during the healing period. A back brace serves several critical functions during this time. It limits the range of motion of your spine, preventing movements that could stress the surgical repair. It distributes load away from the surgical site, reducing the forces acting on healing tissues. And it provides proprioceptive feedback, a constant physical reminder to maintain proper posture and avoid problematic movements.

The specific type of brace prescribed depends on the nature and location of your surgery. Patients who have undergone lumbar fusion typically receive an LSO or TLSO brace. Patients with thoracic procedures may receive a TLSO or a Jewett hyperextension brace. Your surgeon selects the brace that provides the appropriate level and location of support for your specific surgical repair.

Getting the Right Fit

A properly fitted brace is essential. When your brace is first fitted, it should feel snug but not painfully tight. You should be able to breathe normally and should not have any pressure points that dig into your skin. The brace should sit level, not tilting to one side or the other, and should remain in position when you move. If your brace shifts, slides, or creates painful pressure points, contact your provider for an adjustment before continuing to wear it.

Most braces have adjustable components that allow for changes as your recovery progresses. Swelling may decrease in the weeks after surgery, requiring tightening of the brace. Weight changes, muscle atrophy, or changes in posture may also necessitate adjustments. Do not attempt to modify the structural components of your brace yourself. Contact your orthotic provider or your surgeon's office for any needed adjustments.

Daily Living With Your Brace

During your bracing period, you will wear the brace whenever you are upright, meaning any time you are sitting, standing, or walking. Most patients are permitted to remove the brace when lying flat in bed, though your surgeon may specify otherwise. When putting on the brace, most patients find it easiest to lie down, position the brace around their torso, secure the closures, and then stand up.

Clothing is best worn under the brace. A thin, smooth-fitting shirt or undershirt helps prevent skin irritation and absorbs perspiration. Avoid clothing with thick seams, buttons, or zippers at the contact points. Check your skin daily for any red marks, pressure sores, or irritation, and report any skin breakdown to your healthcare provider promptly.

Activity and Movement

Your surgeon will provide specific guidelines about what activities are and are not permitted during your bracing period. In general, walking is encouraged as it promotes circulation, prevents deconditioning, and supports overall recovery. Bending, twisting, and lifting are typically restricted. Getting in and out of bed, chairs, and cars requires modified techniques that your clinical team should demonstrate before you are discharged.

Physical therapy usually begins during the bracing period, with exercises specifically designed to be performed while wearing the brace. These typically include gentle range-of-motion exercises for the extremities, breathing exercises, and progressive walking programs. Your physical therapist will work within the restrictions established by your surgeon and your brace.

Duration and Transition

The duration of post-surgical bracing varies based on the procedure performed and the progress of healing. Spinal fusions typically require bracing for 8 to 12 weeks, though some may be longer. Fracture bracing may last 8 to 16 weeks depending on the fracture type and healing progress. Your surgeon will make the determination about when you are ready to discontinue bracing based on clinical examination and imaging studies.

When the time comes to stop wearing your brace, the transition is usually gradual. Many surgeons recommend a weaning period where brace use is progressively reduced over one to two weeks rather than stopped abruptly. This allows the muscles that have been partially supported by the brace to gradually resume their full load-bearing role.

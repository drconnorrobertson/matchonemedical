DME Billing and Compliance: What Practices Need to Know

March 14, 2026 — DME Products

For medical practices that dispense durable medical equipment as part of their patient care, understanding the billing, coding, and compliance landscape is essential. Proper DME billing ensures that practices receive appropriate reimbursement for the devices they provide, that patients are not exposed to unexpected financial obligations, and that the practice operates within the regulatory framework established by Medicare, Medicaid, and commercial insurance programs.

Coding Fundamentals

DME items are coded using the Healthcare Common Procedure Coding System (HCPCS) Level II codes. Each type of device has a specific code that identifies it for billing purposes. Thermal compression devices, microcurrent units, back braces, and other DME items each have designated codes that must be used correctly on claims submissions. Using incorrect codes can result in claim denials, delayed payments, and potential compliance issues.

In addition to HCPCS codes, DME billing requires appropriate International Classification of Diseases (ICD-10) diagnosis codes that document the medical condition for which the device is being prescribed. The diagnosis code must support the medical necessity of the prescribed device. A thermal compression device prescribed for post-surgical knee swelling, for example, requires a diagnosis code that reflects the surgical procedure and the resulting condition, not just a general pain code.

Documentation Requirements

Adequate documentation is the foundation of compliant DME billing. For every device dispensed, the practice must maintain a physician prescription or order that specifies the device and the diagnosis, clinical notes from the face-to-face encounter supporting medical necessity, documentation of proper delivery and fitting of the device, and evidence that the patient was instructed in the proper use and care of the device.

Medicare and most commercial payers require that the medical necessity for the device be clearly documented in the physician's clinical notes from the encounter that gave rise to the DME order. This documentation must establish the patient's diagnosis, describe the functional limitation the device is intended to address, and explain why the prescribed device is the appropriate treatment for the condition. Generic statements like the patient needs a brace are insufficient; the documentation must be specific and individualized.

Compliance Considerations

DME billing is subject to scrutiny from Medicare Administrative Contractors, commercial insurance audit departments, and federal enforcement agencies including the Office of Inspector General. Practices that dispense DME must ensure that their billing practices comply with all applicable regulations, including the Anti-Kickback Statute, which prohibits offering or receiving anything of value in exchange for referrals of DME, the Stark Law, which restricts physician self-referrals for certain designated health services, and the False Claims Act, which imposes severe penalties for knowingly submitting false claims to federal healthcare programs.

Compliance programs should include written policies and procedures for DME billing, regular training for staff involved in the ordering, dispensing, and billing process, internal audits to identify and correct billing errors, and clear protocols for responding to audit requests from payers. The Office of Inspector General publishes guidance specifically for DME suppliers that provides a roadmap for building an effective compliance program.

Common Billing Pitfalls

Several common mistakes can create compliance problems and revenue leakage for DME-dispensing practices. Insufficient documentation is the most frequent issue, with practices failing to maintain adequate records of medical necessity, device delivery, and patient education. Incorrect coding, including the use of codes that do not match the device actually provided, is another common error. Failure to verify patient insurance eligibility and coverage before dispensing devices can result in uncollectable patient balances and claim denials.

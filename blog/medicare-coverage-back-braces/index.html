Medicare Coverage for Back Braces: A Patient's Guide to Benefits

March 25, 2026 — Back Bracing

For Medicare beneficiaries dealing with spinal conditions, understanding how Medicare covers back braces and orthotics is essential for accessing the care they need without unexpected financial burden. Medicare does cover medically necessary back braces, but specific eligibility criteria, documentation requirements, and coverage limitations apply. This guide explains what Medicare patients need to know about obtaining a covered back brace.

Medicare Coverage Basics for Back Braces

Back braces fall under Medicare Part B as Durable Medical Equipment (DME). Medicare Part B covers DME that is medically necessary and prescribed by a physician. For a back brace to be covered, it must be prescribed by a Medicare-enrolled physician, be medically necessary for the treatment of the patient's diagnosed condition, be supplied by a Medicare-enrolled DME supplier, and meet the specific coverage criteria outlined in the applicable Local Coverage Determination (LCD).

Medicare typically covers 80 percent of the Medicare-approved amount for the brace after the patient has met their Part B deductible. The remaining 20 percent is the patient's responsibility and may be covered by a Medigap supplemental insurance policy if the patient has one. Medicare Advantage plans may have different cost-sharing arrangements, so patients enrolled in these plans should check with their specific plan for details.

Medical Necessity Requirements

Medicare's definition of medical necessity for back braces requires documentation that the brace is being prescribed to treat a specific, documented medical condition. Common qualifying diagnoses include vertebral fractures, post-surgical stabilization, degenerative disc disease with documented functional limitation, spinal stenosis with documented symptoms, and scoliosis. The prescribing physician must document the diagnosis, the functional limitation the brace is intended to address, and the expected therapeutic benefit.

General back pain without a specific structural diagnosis is typically not sufficient to meet Medicare's medical necessity threshold for a rigid or semi-rigid orthosis. However, appropriate diagnostic evaluation often reveals specific structural findings that do qualify for brace coverage. Working with a knowledgeable prescriber who understands Medicare documentation requirements is important for ensuring appropriate coverage.

The Ordering and Documentation Process

A face-to-face encounter with the prescribing physician is required before a back brace can be ordered for a Medicare patient. During this encounter, the physician examines the patient, confirms the diagnosis, determines that bracing is medically appropriate, and documents their findings. The resulting documentation must clearly support the medical necessity of the prescribed brace and must be maintained in the patient's medical record.

The physician then provides a prescription, sometimes called an order or a Certificate of Medical Necessity, that specifies the type of brace, the diagnosis, and any special requirements. This prescription is provided to the DME supplier who will furnish the brace. The supplier is responsible for verifying Medicare eligibility, confirming coverage, fitting the brace, and billing Medicare appropriately.

What Medicare Pays For

Medicare covers both off-the-shelf and custom-fabricated back braces when they meet medical necessity criteria. Off-the-shelf braces are pre-manufactured devices that may be adjusted for individual fit but are not custom-made for a specific patient. Custom-fabricated braces are made specifically for an individual patient based on their measurements or casts. Custom braces are covered when the patient's condition or anatomy requires a level of customization that cannot be achieved with an off-the-shelf device.

Medicare also covers replacement of a brace when the existing device is worn out, damaged beyond repair, or no longer fits due to a significant change in the patient's condition. Replacement at reasonable intervals, typically not more frequently than every three years, is generally covered when documented medical need exists.
